\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem Background}

With the increasing penetration of distributed renewable energy, microgrids have become an important component of modern power systems. A typical grid-connected microgrid integrates residential loads, photovoltaic generation, battery energy storage, and the external power grid. Although photovoltaic generation can reduce dependence on external electricity, its output varies over time and may not always meet the residential demand. Battery storage therefore plays an important role in balancing local supply and demand and improving the utilization of renewable energy.

In a grid-connected microgrid, electricity can be purchased from the external grid when local photovoltaic generation and stored energy are insufficient. Since the electricity price varies with time, the timing of grid purchases and battery charging or discharging directly affects the total operating cost. During low-price periods, the microgrid may purchase additional electricity for storage, while during high-price periods, the battery can release previously stored energy to reduce expensive grid purchases.

In practical operation, the scheduling problem becomes more challenging because residential demand, photovoltaic generation, and electricity prices may vary over time and cannot always be known exactly in advance. Consequently, an effective microgrid scheduling strategy should coordinate grid purchases and battery operation under both deterministic and uncertain conditions, while ensuring that the residential electricity demand is satisfied and the total electricity purchasing cost is minimized.

\subsection{Restatement of the Problem}

The four tasks seek purchasing and battery schedules that satisfy residential demand and minimize electricity expenditure under the specified operating and contract rules.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.35em,parsep=0pt,topsep=0.35em]
\item \textbf{Problem 1:}
Using the fixed daily load and tariff profiles and the supplied PV forecast, determine the day's schedule at 00:00. Battery energy at 24:00 must equal its initial level.

\item \textbf{Problem 2:}
With varying load and PV generation but the same daily tariff, determine a purchase commitment at each midnight. Committed purchases are paid in full, and any residual shortage is covered by emergency purchases at five times the normal price.

\item \textbf{Problem 3:}
Use the PV forecasts released at 00:00, 06:00, 12:00, and 18:00 to set and revise the daily purchase plan. Include emergency costs, the 50\% charge on cancelled purchases, and the 1.5-times price for additional purchases; assess whether further forecast-release times are needed.

\item \textbf{Problem 4:}
Recalculate the day-ahead and rolling strategies of Problems 2 and 3 using the supplied fluctuating-price data, and report their purchasing schedules and costs.
\end{itemize}

For each task, report the required tabulated results in the paper and export the complete strategies using the prescribed workbook templates.

\section{Model Assumptions}

To simplify the microgrid scheduling problem while preserving its main operational characteristics, the following assumptions are adopted throughout the study.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Complete satisfaction of electricity demand.}
    The residential electricity demand must be fully satisfied in every scheduling interval. Any shortage that cannot be covered by photovoltaic generation, battery discharge, or planned grid purchases is supplied through emergency electricity purchases when applicable.

    \item \textbf{Unidirectional interaction with the external grid.}
    The microgrid can purchase electricity from the external grid but cannot sell electricity back to it. Since no grid-connection capacity limit is specified, the external grid is assumed to have sufficient capacity to provide the required electricity.

    \item \textbf{Local utilization of photovoltaic generation.}
    Photovoltaic generation is first used within the microgrid to satisfy residential demand or charge the battery. If the available photovoltaic energy exceeds the local demand and available charging capacity, the excessive energy should be discarded.

    \item \textbf{Piecewise-constant operation within each interval.}
    Electricity price, residential load, photovoltaic generation, and battery operating decisions are assumed to remain constant within each scheduling interval. Therefore, power quantities can be converted into energy according to the corresponding interval length.

    \item \textbf{Cost components.}
    Only the electricity-related costs specified in each problem are considered. These include planned grid purchases, emergency purchases, and purchasing-plan adjustment costs where applicable. 

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem Analysis}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.35em,parsep=0pt,topsep=0.35em]
\item \textbf{Problem 1.}
The central difficulty is allocating energy across time: charging now changes the battery's ability to avoid expensive purchases later. Conversion losses, capacity limits, and the terminal-energy requirement restrict this flexibility. Treating the supplied PV forecast as a deterministic input makes these relationships linear, motivating a linear program for joint purchasing and storage scheduling.

\item \textbf{Problem 2.}
Forecast errors have asymmetric consequences: under-purchasing can trigger expensive emergency supply, while over-purchasing can leave paid-for surplus. Their duration and timing also determine whether storage can absorb the deviations. Historical net-load paths therefore provide the uncertainty representation for a scenario-tree purchasing model. Separating advance commitments from actual battery feedback allows the strategy to be evaluated under realized demand rather than forecast trajectories alone.

\item \textbf{Problem 3.}
New forecasts create opportunities to correct earlier commitments, but an adjustment is worthwhile only when its expected benefit outweighs its cost. Rolling stochastic optimization can resolve this trade-off by updating the unexecuted schedule with newly available information and the observed storage state. The analysis should assess the value of the supplied releases and the potential benefit of additional forecast times.

\item \textbf{Problem 4.}
Price uncertainty changes the cost of a net-load forecast error and the value of storing energy for later use. Assuming future prices are unavailable at commitment, causal price forecasts and paired price--net-load residual scenarios extend the earlier models to this joint uncertainty. Forecast quality should then be assessed through purchasing cost as well as prediction error, since accurate prices alone do not guarantee economical scheduling.

\end{itemize}

\section{Deterministic Day-Ahead Scheduling with Battery Storage}


\subsection{Problem Reformulation}

Problem 1 is a deterministic day-ahead energy scheduling problem. 
The 24-hour horizon is divided into 144 consecutive 10-minute intervals. 
For each interval, the electricity price, residential load demand, and photovoltaic generation are assumed to be known in advance. The microgrid must determine the amount of electricity purchased from the external grid together with the charging and discharging schedule of the battery. Therefore, Problem 1 is transformed into a deterministic linear programming problem whose objective is to minimize the total daily electricity purchasing cost while satisfying the energy balance and the operational constraints of the battery.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

The original residential load and photovoltaic generation data are given in terms of power (kW), whereas the scheduling decisions are expressed in terms of energy (kWh). Since each scheduling interval lasts 10 minutes, we set $\Delta t=\frac{1}{6}\ \text{h}$.

Accordingly, the residential load and available photovoltaic energy in interval $t$ are converted as
\[
L_t=P_t^{L}\Delta t,
\qquad
R_t=P_t^{PV}\Delta t.
\]


The same conversion is applied to the battery charging and discharging limits. With a maximum charging/discharging power of $5000$ kW, the maximum transferable energy in one interval is $5000\times(1/6)=833.33$ kWh. After preprocessing, all energy-related quantities are expressed in kWh and aligned over the same 144 ten-minute scheduling intervals for subsequent optimization.

\subsection{Linear Programming Model}

For Problem 1, the day index $d$ introduced in Table~\ref{tab:variables} is omitted because only one representative day is considered. The decision variables are the grid purchase $G_t$, battery charging energy $C_t$, battery discharging energy $D_t$, curtailed photovoltaic energy $W_t$, and battery energy level $S_t$.

\subsubsection{Objective Function}

The objective is to minimize the total daily electricity purchasing cost:

\[
\min J=\sum_{t=1}^{144} p_t G_t.
\]

\subsubsection{Energy Balance Constraint}

For each scheduling interval, the total electricity supply must equal the total electricity consumption. Therefore,

\[
G_t+R_t+D_t
=
L_t+C_t+W_t,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144.
\]

The left-hand side $G_t$, $R_t$, and $D_t$ represent electricity purchased from the external grid, available photovoltaic energy, and battery discharge, respectively. The right-hand side $L_t$, $C_t$, and $W_t$ represent residential demand, battery charging, and curtailed photovoltaic energy. Since electricity selling to the external grid is not considered, photovoltaic energy that cannot be consumed or stored is represented by $W_t$.
\subsubsection{Battery Dynamics}

Considering the charging and discharging efficiencies, the battery energy evolves according to

\[
S_{t+1}
=
S_t+\eta_c C_t-\frac{D_t}{\eta_d},
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144,
\]

where $\eta_c=\eta_d=0.90$.

This constraint links the battery energy levels of consecutive scheduling intervals.


\subsubsection{Battery Capacity and Power Constraints}

The battery energy must remain within its allowable operating range:

\[
1200\le S_t\le10800,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,145.
\]

Since the maximum charging and discharging power is $5000$ kW, the corresponding maximum energy transfer in one 10-minute interval is $833.33$ kWh. Therefore,

\[
0\le C_t\le833.33,
\qquad
0\le D_t\le833.33,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144.
\]

In addition,

\[
G_t\ge0,
\qquad
W_t\ge0,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144.
\]

\subsubsection{Initial and Terminal Conditions}

The initial battery energy at 0:00 is $S_1=6000\ \text{kWh}$.

According to the requirement that the battery energy at 24:00 equals that at 0:00, $S_{145}=S_1=6000\ \text{kWh}$.

This terminal condition prevents the model from reducing the daily purchasing cost simply by exhausting the battery at the end of the day.

\subsubsection{Complete LP Formulation}

Combining the objective function and all operational constraints, the complete linear programming model for Problem 1 is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\min \quad
& J=\sum_{t=1}^{144} p_t G_t \\[2mm]
\text{s.t.}\quad
& G_t+R_t+D_t=L_t+C_t+W_t,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& S_{t+1}=S_t+\eta_c C_t-\frac{D_t}{\eta_d},
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& \eta_c=\eta_d=0.9,\\
& 1200\le S_t\le10800,
&& t=1,\ldots,145,\\
& 0\le C_t\le833.33,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& 0\le D_t\le833.33,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& G_t\ge0,\quad W_t\ge0,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& S_1=S_{145}=6000.
\end{aligned}
\]

All decision variables are continuous, and both the objective function and constraints are linear. Therefore, the resulting optimization problem is a standard linear programming problem.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

The proposed linear programming model was implemented in Python and solved using the HiGHS solver through \texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog}. The solver successfully reached the optimal solution while satisfying all energy balance and battery operating constraints.

\subsubsection{Discussion}

The optimal strategy demonstrates the role of the battery in shifting electricity consumption across different price periods. During low-price periods, additional electricity is purchased and stored in the battery. When the electricity price rises, the stored energy is released to reduce expensive grid purchases. This behavior is particularly evident during the afternoon and evening peak-price periods, when substantial battery discharge occurs.

The stored energy remains within the prescribed range of 1200--10800 kWh throughout the day. The battery reaches relatively high energy levels after periods of charging and is gradually discharged when electricity prices increase. Near the end of the day, the battery is recharged so that its energy returns to 6000 kWh at 24:00.

Overall, the obtained schedule reflects a typical peak-shaving and valley-filling strategy: the microgrid shifts electricity purchases from high-price periods to low-price periods through battery storage while maintaining the required energy balance and operational constraints.

\section{Scenario-Tree Day-Ahead Scheduling under Net-Load Uncertainty}
\label{sec:model2}

Unlike Problem 1, where purchases and battery actions are jointly scheduled under deterministic inputs, Problem 2 commits purchases before load and PV generation are realized. Historical forecasts and residual paths form a scenario tree for choosing the midnight commitment; actual battery operation follows a feedback rule, with remaining deficits covered by emergency purchases at five times the normal tariff. We first define the available information, then formulate purchasing and execution, and finally evaluate realized costs.

\subsection{Information Boundary and Uncertainty Modeling}

\subsubsection{Decision and Time Conventions}
\label{sec:q2-time}

Net-load power is $P^N_{d,t}=P^L_{d,t}-P^{PV}_{d,t}=(L_{d,t}-R_{d,t})/\Delta t$ in kW, with $p_t=p_{d,t}$ fixed across days. Charging and discharging energies are measured on the microgrid side; $W_{d,t}$ denotes all unused surplus, including purchased energy. The inherited storage limits are $S_{\min}=1200$, $S_{\max}=10800$ kWh, $\eta_c=\eta_d=0.9$, and $\bar C=\bar D=5000\Delta t=2500/3$ kWh.

To match the 144 workbook columns, we assume source timestamps are interval left endpoints. Purchase row $d$ covers $d$ 00:10 to $(d+1)$ 00:10. A civil day combines the preceding row's last interval with the current row's first 143 intervals. At midnight, the last interval of row $d-1$ is still pending; its measurement is excluded from forecasting. The first unrepresented interval, January 1 at 00:00--00:10, retains the specified 6000 kWh initial state. Actual settlement is
\begin{equation}
J=\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}\sum_{t=1}^{144}p_t\bigl(G_{d,t}+5E_{d,t}\bigr),
\label{eq:q2-settlement}
\end{equation}
with committed purchases paid in full and no credit for unused energy or final inventory.

\subsubsection{Central Forecast and Daily Residual Scenarios}

The central forecast combines last week's load with the median recent PV output. Available PV rows are
\[
\mathcal A_{d,t}=\begin{cases}
\{d-7,\ldots,d-1\},&t=1,\ldots,143,\\
\{d-7,\ldots,d-2\},&t=144.
\end{cases}
\]
giving
\begin{equation}
\widehat P^{N,0}_{d,t}
=P^L_{d-7,t}
-\operatorname{median}\{P^{PV}_{j,t}:j\in\mathcal A_{d,t}\}.
\label{eq:q2-central-forecast}
\end{equation}
Complete residual paths retain deficit duration and timing relevant to storage. For historical row $j$, define the retrospective reference and residual
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 B^N_{j,t}&=P^L_{j-7,t}
 -\operatorname{median}\{P^{PV}_{j-7,t},\ldots,P^{PV}_{j-1,t}\},\\
 r_{j,t}&=P^{N,\mathrm{act}}_{j,t}-B^N_{j,t}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q2-residual}
\end{equation}
Eligible rows are $\mathcal H_d=\{\max(7,d-56),\ldots,d-2\}$, indexing January 1 by zero. Their complete residuals are known at decision $d$. The historical reference uses seven preceding rows, so its last-slot median differs from the six-row median available at historical midnight. We select same-weekday rows first, newest first, then fill the twelve places with the most recent other eligible rows:
\begin{equation}
P^{N,(s)}_{d,t}=\widehat P^{N,0}_{d,t}+r_{j_s,t},
\qquad s=1,\ldots,12,\qquad \pi_s=\frac1{12}.
\label{eq:q2-scenarios}
\end{equation}
The 56-row look-back window is shortened when less history is available; equal weights represent the empirical scenario distribution.

\subsection{Day-Ahead Optimization and Feedback Execution}

\subsubsection{Scenario-Tree Purchasing Model}

We divide each purchase row into six four-hour stages starting at 00:10, 04:10, and so on. All scenarios share the initial node. At each later boundary, two-cluster K-means splits each parent node using its preceding-stage net-load paths; singleton nodes remain unchanged. Each stage has at most twelve nodes.

Let $\mathcal T_k=\{24(k-1)+1,\ldots,24k\}$ and let $s\sim_k s'$ mean that two scenarios share a stage-$k$ node. We impose
\begin{equation}
C^{(s)}_{d,t}=C^{(s')}_{d,t},\qquad
D^{(s)}_{d,t}=D^{(s')}_{d,t},
\quad t\in\mathcal T_k,\quad s\sim_k s'.
\label{eq:q2-nonanticipativity}
\end{equation}
These nodewise constraints allow storage actions to adapt as histories separate, enforcing nonanticipativity on the coarse tree \cite{birge2011}. Purchases $G_{d,t}$ are shared by all scenarios.

With $\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t}$ denoting scenario shortage, the planning problem is
\begin{equation}
\min\
\sum_t p_tG_{d,t}
+\lambda\sum_s\pi_s\sum_t5p_t\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t}
+\epsilon\sum_s\pi_s\sum_t(C^{(s)}_{d,t}+D^{(s)}_{d,t}),
\label{eq:q2-objective}
\end{equation}
subject to \eqref{eq:q2-nonanticipativity} and, for every scenario and interval,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G_{d,t}+D^{(s)}_{d,t}+\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t}
&=\Delta tP^{N,(s)}_{d,t}+C^{(s)}_{d,t}+W^{(s)}_{d,t},\\
S^{(s)}_{d,t}
&=S^{(s)}_{d,t-1}+\eta_cC^{(s)}_{d,t}-D^{(s)}_{d,t}/\eta_d,\\
S_{\min}\le S^{(s)}_{d,t}&\le S_{\max},\\
0\le C^{(s)}_{d,t}&\le\bar C,\qquad 0\le D^{(s)}_{d,t}\le\bar D,\\
G_{d,t},\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t},W^{(s)}_{d,t}&\ge0,\\
S^{(s)}_{d,0}&=S_{d,0},\qquad S^{(s)}_{d,144}\ge S_{\mathrm{res}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q2-planning-constraints}
\end{equation}
The planner initializes $S_{d,0}$ with observed midnight storage, approximating the 00:10 state by omitting the pending interval. The reserve $S_{\mathrm{res}}=3000$ kWh constrains scenario storage at next-day 00:10, rather than actual civil-midnight storage. We use $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ CNY/kWh to regularize throughput; the shortage weight $\lambda$ sets the internal price $5\lambda p_t$.

HiGHS solves this linear program \cite{huangfu2018}. Shortage and surplus may vary within a node, and the surrogate permits simultaneous charging/discharging and emergency-assisted charging. Only its purchase schedule is implemented; the following feedback rule enforces physical operation.

\subsubsection{Causal Execution and Settlement}
\label{sec:q2-execution}

For a physical interval $i$ assigned to purchase-row entry $(d,t)$, let $S_i^{-}$ be its actual initial stored energy and define
\[
N^{\mathrm{act}}_{d,t}=\Delta tP^{N,\mathrm{act}}_{d,t},
\qquad M_{d,t}=G_{d,t}-N^{\mathrm{act}}_{d,t}.
\]
For $M_{d,t}\ge0$, the battery absorbs the available surplus:
\begin{equation}
C_{d,t}=\min\left\{M_{d,t},\bar C,
\frac{S_{\max}-S_i^{-}}{\eta_c}\right\},\quad
D_{d,t}=E_{d,t}=0,\quad W_{d,t}=M_{d,t}-C_{d,t}.
\label{eq:q2-feedback-surplus}
\end{equation}
For $M_{d,t}<0$, it supplies as much of the deficit as its limits allow:
\begin{equation}
D_{d,t}=\min\left\{-M_{d,t},\bar D,
\eta_d(S_i^{-}-S_{\min})\right\},\quad
C_{d,t}=W_{d,t}=0,\quad E_{d,t}=-M_{d,t}-D_{d,t}.
\label{eq:q2-feedback-deficit}
\end{equation}
The realized storage state is advanced in physical chronological order:
\begin{equation}
S_i^{+}=S_i^{-}+\eta_cC_{d,t}-D_{d,t}/\eta_d,
\qquad S_{i+1}^{-}=S_i^{+}.
\label{eq:q2-actual-storage}
\end{equation}
Charging and discharging are mutually exclusive, and emergency purchases cover only the remaining deficit. At each midnight, the controller commits the new purchase row before executing the preceding row's pending interval, then executes the current row's first 143 intervals. Storage carries forward continuously, and \eqref{eq:q2-settlement} evaluates the realized flows.

\subsection{Scenario-Tree Day-Ahead Scheduling under Fluctuating~Prices}
\label{sec:q4-2}

\subsubsection{Optimization and Settlement}

Scheduled purchases are shared across scenarios. The six-stage tree remains based on net-load histories, and storage actions satisfy \eqref{eq:q2-nonanticipativity}. Realized day-ahead expenditure is
\begin{equation}
J^{4\text{-}2}
=\sum_{d\in\mathcal D}\sum_t p^{\mathrm{act}}_{d,t}
\bigl(G_{d,t}+5E_{d,t}\bigr).
\label{eq:q4-2-settlement}
\end{equation}

The daily price-aware objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min\quad
&\sum_t\left(\sum_s\pi_sp^{(s)}_{d,t}\right)G_{d,t}
+\lambda\sum_s\pi_s\sum_t5p^{(s)}_{d,t}\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t}\\
&\quad+\epsilon\sum_s\pi_s\sum_t
\left(C^{(s)}_{d,t}+D^{(s)}_{d,t}\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q4-2-objective}
\end{equation}
subject to \eqref{eq:q2-nonanticipativity} and \eqref{eq:q2-planning-constraints}. We retain $\lambda=0.9$, $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ CNY/kWh, and the 3000 kWh scenario terminal reserve. Thus planning shortages carry coefficient $4.5p^{(s)}_{d,t}$, while actual emergency purchases cost $5p^{\mathrm{act}}_{d,t}$. HiGHS solves the linear program \cite{huangfu2018}.

Only the purchase schedule is passed to the feedback controller in Section~\ref{sec:q2-execution}. Each policy uses the same realized net load and price data, but its commitments generate its own storage and emergency-purchase trajectory. A perfect-price benchmark repeats the realized price path in all twelve scenarios, holding the net-load scenarios and controller fixed. This hindsight case measures the value of price information within the tested framework, rather than providing a global optimal-cost bound.

\subsection{Rolling Multistage Scenario-Tree Scheduling under Fluctuating Prices}
\label{sec:q4-3}

\subsubsection{Rolling Forecasts and Paired Scenarios}

Q4--3 extends the rolling purchasing framework of Problem 3 to joint net-load and electricity-price uncertainty. Unlike the two-stage recourse approximation used in Problem 3, the Q4--3 planning model imposes four-hour scenario-tree nonanticipativity constraints on battery charging and discharging decisions. This extension therefore changes both the price representation and the planning recourse structure, rather than constituting a price-only recalculation.

Purchase revisions remain common across scenarios within each optimization and are recomputed at the available forecast-release times. Only the revised purchase schedule is passed to the physical feedback controller; the scenario battery trajectories are not directly executed.

Decisions are updated at $k\in\{0,6,12,18\}$ hours, using the latest Attachment-3 PV forecast and a load forecast corrected by demand already observed. The planning model uses twelve paired residual paths and a 3000 kWh terminal reserve.

Write $h_t=t/6$ for the left-endpoint time in hours after midnight of purchase-row interval $t$, and let $\mathcal H_k$ contain the remaining intervals. The midnight price forecast is the four-week weekday mean in \eqref{eq:q4-2-price-forecast}. At $k>0$, let $\mathcal O_{d,k}$ contain completed physical intervals in the preceding six hours, assembled by civil time as in Section~\ref{sec:q2-time}, and let $e^{p,0}_i$ be their observed price error relative to the midnight forecast.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\noindent The remaining price path is corrected by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b_{d,k}&=\operatorname{median}_{i\in\mathcal O_{d,k}}e^{p,0}_i,\\
\widehat p_{d,k,t}&=\max\left\{0,\widehat p_{d,0,t}
+b_{d,k}\exp\left[-\frac{h_t-k}{6}\right]\right\},
\quad t\in\mathcal H_k.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q4-3-price-update}
\end{equation}
Here $\widehat p_{d,0,t}=\widehat p_{d,t}$, and both the decay scale and time differences are measured in hours. Only completed observations before the issue boundary enter the correction. Historical forecasts are reconstructed at the corresponding issue time to obtain paired net-load and price residuals for the remaining horizon. Net-load energy is $N^{(s)}_{d,k,t}=\Delta tP^{N,(s)}_{d,k,t}$, consistent with Model 2's power notation.

\subsubsection{Purchase Revisions and Realized Settlement}

At midnight, the price-aware objective \eqref{eq:q4-2-objective} produces a baseline $q^0_{d,t}$ using the initial Q4--3 forecasts. At each later issue, upward and downward revisions are measured against that baseline:
\begin{equation}
q^k_{d,t}=q^0_{d,t}+u^k_{d,t}-v^k_{d,t}\ge0,
\qquad u^k_{d,t},v^k_{d,t}\ge0,
\quad t\in\mathcal H_k.
\label{eq:q4-3-revision}
\end{equation}
With $\bar p_{d,k,t}=\sum_s\pi_sp^{(s)}_{d,k,t}$, the remaining-horizon problem is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min\quad
&\sum_{t\in\mathcal H_k}\bar p_{d,k,t}
\left(1.5u^k_{d,t}-0.5v^k_{d,t}\right)\\
&+4.5\sum_s\pi_s\sum_{t\in\mathcal H_k}
p^{(s)}_{d,k,t}\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,k,t}\\
&+10^{-4}\sum_s\pi_s\sum_{t\in\mathcal H_k}
\left(C^{(s)}_{d,k,t}+D^{(s)}_{d,k,t}\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q4-3-objective}
\end{equation}
The energy-balance and storage constraints in \eqref{eq:q2-planning-constraints} apply on the remaining horizon with $G$ replaced by $q^k$, the updated net-load scenarios, and the observed storage state at the issue boundary. The midnight initialization follows Section~\ref{sec:q2-time}. Storage actions satisfy the four-hour tree constraints, and terminal scenario energy remains at least 3000 kWh. The baseline purchase term is constant at a revision and is omitted from \eqref{eq:q4-3-objective}.

Each solution updates the unexecuted tail; purchases up to the next issue are implemented with the feedback controller in Section~\ref{sec:q2-execution}. Let $u_{d,t},v_{d,t}$ be the revisions associated with the final implemented purchase for interval $t$. Each interval is settled once against the midnight baseline:
\begin{equation}
J^{4\text{-}3}=\sum_{d,t}p^{\mathrm{act}}_{d,t}
\left(q^0_{d,t}+1.5u_{d,t}-0.5v_{d,t}+5E_{d,t}\right).
\label{eq:q4-3-settlement}
\end{equation}
Thus, cancelling $v$ kWh leaves half of its baseline charge payable, while additional energy costs $1.5p^{\mathrm{act}}$ per kWh. Figure~\ref{fig:q4-f4} shows the issued price forecasts and the resulting change from the initial to final purchase schedule.

\subsection{Strengths}

The framework separates advance commitments from realized operation and uses a common physical simulator and settlement rule. Complete daily residual paths retain temporal dependence relevant to storage, while nodewise constraints limit premature scenario adaptation. This provides an interpretable connection between uncertainty and purchasing decisions.

Problem 4 extends this separation to forecast, scenario, and realized prices. Pairing historical price and net-load residuals represents their empirical dependence, while the common feedback controller permits a consistent comparison of price-driven purchase policies. The results distinguish prediction accuracy, emergency volume, and total operating cost as separate evaluation criteria. The fixed-structure Q4--3 update experiment quantifies the benefit of additional decision opportunities within the rolling contract (Section~\ref{sec:q4-3-updates}).

\subsection{Limitations and Possible Improvements}
\label{sec:q2-limitations}

For Problem 2, limitations concern both uncertainty representation and planning accuracy. The finite residual set excludes weather predictors, and its retrospective reference differs at the last slot from the forecast that would have been available at each historical midnight. The surrogate also approximates the 00:10 initial state and permits storage trajectories that differ from feedback execution; its terminal reserve is not a realized daily constraint. Concrete improvements are to model the pending interval using only information available at midnight, align historical residual references with the issued forecasting rule, and test tighter planning--execution consistency. These changes require recomputation, not relabeling of existing results. The adopted source timestamp semantics remain an assumption, and evaluation-period parameter selection requires separate testing with frozen settings before claims of generalization.

For Problem 4, one year of price history, equal-weight scenarios, and absent exogenous price drivers limit the representation of uncertainty. Price-model selection uses the reporting year, so additional years are needed for independent testing and probability calibration. Q4--3 adopts the four-hour tree; a matched tree-versus-relaxation experiment under fluctuating prices would help assess this structural choice. \subsection{Detailed Validation of the Final Policy}
\label{app:q2-validation}

\textbf{January warm-up.}
Storage starts at 6000 kWh on January 1, 2025, with zero purchases that day. Subsequent January purchase rows use the nonnegative part of the previous row's net load, converted to energy. The pending last slot is replaced by the most recent complete row's value, or zero on January 2. The feedback controller carries storage forward to the common February 1 midnight state of 10800 kWh.

\textbf{Realized feasibility.}
Across 48,096 settled intervals, the maximum energy-balance residual is $1.14\times10^{-13}$ kWh and inter-row storage-linking error is zero. Storage respects 1200--10800 kWh, with mutually exclusive charging/discharging and no emergency-assisted charging. Final storage is 3019.16 kWh at 00:10 on January 1, 2026; 74 civil-day endings fall below the 3000 kWh planning reserve. These realized outcomes are distinct from the scenario diagnostics below.

\textbf{Planning--execution diagnostics.}
The saved surrogate trajectories contain no simultaneous charge/discharge, but 15,200 scenario intervals contain both charging and a positive shortage variable. These trajectories are not physically executed. Replaying all twelve scenarios on each evaluation row with the actual feedback rule gives 478 terminal states below the planning reserve out of 4,008 scenario-row replays. The maximum absolute difference between planned and replayed terminal energy is 5392.32 kWh.

Each replay uses the same purchase row, scenario, and nominal initial state as its planning problem. The maximum absolute per-scenario purchase-plus-shortage cost difference is CNY 21,650.08, evaluated with the internal $4.5p_t$ shortage coefficient on both paths and excluding the throughput regularizer. This is neither an actual $5p_t$ settlement difference nor the gap between optimized expected objectives. These separate row-level replays do not constitute a continuously simulated alternative year. They quantify the surrogate approximation rather than provide an independent performance test.

\textbf{Information-availability tests.}
The implementation observes storage before executing the pending midnight interval and excludes that interval's unavailable measurement from forecasting. Targeted tests perturb entry 144 of row $d-1$ as well as rows $d$ onward; scenarios and the midnight purchase plans remain unchanged in the recorded tests. The comparison suite reports the corresponding purchase-plan boundary checks for all six policies. A separate within-day test changes later realized measurements and leaves earlier feedback actions unchanged. These checks support the implemented information ordering under the adopted timestamp convention, not the timestamp assumption itself or generalization to an unseen period.
