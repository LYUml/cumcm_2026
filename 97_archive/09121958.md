\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem Background}

With the increasing penetration of distributed renewable energy, microgrids have become an important component of modern power systems. A typical grid-connected microgrid integrates residential loads, photovoltaic generation, battery energy storage, and the external power grid. Although photovoltaic generation can reduce dependence on external electricity, its output varies over time and may not always meet the residential demand. Battery storage therefore plays an important role in balancing local supply and demand and improving the utilization of renewable energy.

In a grid-connected microgrid, electricity can be purchased from the external grid when local photovoltaic generation and stored energy are insufficient. Since the electricity price varies with time, the timing of grid purchases and battery charging or discharging directly affects the total operating cost. During low-price periods, the microgrid may purchase additional electricity for storage, while during high-price periods, the battery can release previously stored energy to reduce expensive grid purchases.

In practical operation, the scheduling problem becomes more challenging because residential demand, photovoltaic generation, and electricity prices may vary over time and cannot always be known exactly in advance. Consequently, an effective microgrid scheduling strategy should coordinate grid purchases and battery operation under both deterministic and uncertain conditions, while ensuring that the residential electricity demand is satisfied and the total electricity purchasing cost is minimized.

\subsection{Restatement of the Problem}

Based on microgrid system, the problem can be divided into the following four tasks.

\textbf{Problem 1:}  
Under the assumption that daily electricity price and residential load pattern remain unchanged, determine the daily electricity purchasing and battery scheduling strategy at 0:00 using given photovoltaic power forecast. Meanwhile, battery energy at 24:00 is required to return to its initial level. The objective is to minimize total daily electricity purchasing cost.

\textbf{Problem 2:}  
Considering residential load and photovoltaic generation vary from day to day, while the daily electricity price pattern remains unchanged, make a purchasing plan at 0:00 each day. If the planned supply is insufficient to meet the actual demand, emergency electricity must be purchased at five times the corresponding electricity price. The task is to develop an appropriate day-ahead scheduling strategy that balances normal purchasing cost and the risk of emergency purchases.

\textbf{Problem 3:}  
Further incorporate photovoltaic forecasts released at 0:00, 6:00, 12:00, and 18:00. The initial purchasing plan is made using the 0:00 forecast and may be adjusted when updated forecasts become available. Reducing the original purchase incurs a penalty equal to 50\% of the corresponding electricity price, while additional purchases are charged at 1.5 times the original price. The objective is to determine an optimal dynamic adjustment strategy considering planned purchases, adjustment costs, and emergency purchases overall, and to evaluate whether additional forecast-update times are necessary.

\textbf{Problem 4:}  
Replace the fixed daily electricity-price pattern with the actual time-varying external-grid prices. Using the given load, photovoltaic, forecast, and electricity-price data, re-evaluate the scheduling strategies in Problems 2 and 3 and analyze how real-time price fluctuations affect the optimal purchasing and battery operation decisions.

\section{Model Assumptions}

To simplify the microgrid scheduling problem while preserving its main operational characteristics, the following assumptions are adopted throughout the study.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Complete satisfaction of electricity demand.}
    The residential electricity demand must be fully satisfied in every scheduling interval. Any shortage that cannot be covered by photovoltaic generation, battery discharge, or planned grid purchases is supplied through emergency electricity purchases when applicable.

    \item \textbf{Unidirectional interaction with the external grid.}
    The microgrid can purchase electricity from the external grid but cannot sell electricity back to it. Since no grid-connection capacity limit is specified, the external grid is assumed to have sufficient capacity to provide the required electricity.

    \item \textbf{Local utilization of photovoltaic generation.}
    Photovoltaic generation is first used within the microgrid to satisfy residential demand or charge the battery. If the available photovoltaic energy exceeds the local demand and available charging capacity, the excessive energy should be discarded.

    \item \textbf{Piecewise-constant operation within each interval.}
    Electricity price, residential load, photovoltaic generation, and battery operating decisions are assumed to remain constant within each scheduling interval. Therefore, power quantities can be converted into energy according to the corresponding interval length.

    \item \textbf{Cost components.}
    Only the electricity-related costs specified in each problem are considered. These include planned grid purchases, emergency purchases, and purchasing-plan adjustment costs where applicable. 

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem Analysis}

\textbf{Problem 1}
The electricity price, residential load, and photovoltaic generation for the entire day are known in advance. Therefore, no forecasting uncertainty needs to be considered. The main task is to coordinate grid purchases with battery charging and discharging while satisfying the energy balance and battery operating constraints. Since both the objective function and the physical constraints are linear, the problem can be formulated as a deterministic linear programming model. This model also provides the basic scheduling framework for the subsequent problems.

\textbf{Problem 2}
introduces daily variations in residential load and photovoltaic generation. The purchasing plan must be determined at 0:00, while deviations between the planned supply and the actual electricity demand may lead to emergency purchases at five times the normal electricity price.  We first estimate the next-day load and photovoltaic generation using historical information, and then use the forecasts to construct the day-ahead purchasing and battery schedule. After the actual load and photovoltaic generation are realized, any remaining electricity shortage is supplied through emergency purchases. 

\subsection{Overall Solution Framework}

\section{Model 1: Solution to Question 1}


\subsection{Problem Reformulation}

Problem 1 is a deterministic day-ahead energy scheduling problem. 
The 24-hour horizon is divided into 144 consecutive 10-minute intervals. 
For each interval, the electricity price, residential load demand, and photovoltaic generation are assumed to be known in advance. The microgrid must determine the amount of electricity purchased from the external grid together with the charging and discharging schedule of the battery. Therefore, Problem 1 is transformed into a deterministic linear programming problem whose objective is to minimize the total daily electricity purchasing cost while satisfying the energy balance and the operational constraints of the battery.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

The original residential load and photovoltaic generation data are given in terms of power (kW), whereas the scheduling decisions are expressed in terms of energy (kWh). Since each scheduling interval lasts 10 minutes, we set
\[
\Delta t=\frac{1}{6}\ \text{h}.
\]

Accordingly, the residential load and available photovoltaic energy in interval $t$ are converted as
\[
L_t=P_t^{L}\Delta t,
\qquad
R_t=P_t^{PV}\Delta t.
\]


The same conversion is applied to the battery charging and discharging limits. With a maximum charging/discharging power of $5000$ kW, the maximum transferable energy in one interval is $5000\times(1/6)=833.33$ kWh. After preprocessing, all energy-related quantities are expressed in kWh and aligned over the same 144 ten-minute scheduling intervals for subsequent optimization.

\subsection{Linear Programming Model}

For Problem 1, the day index $d$ introduced in Section~\ref{tab:variables} is omitted because only one representative day is considered. The decision variables are the grid purchase $G_t$, battery charging energy $C_t$, battery discharging energy $D_t$, curtailed photovoltaic energy $W_t$, and battery energy level $S_t$.

\subsubsection{Objective Function}

The objective is to minimize the total daily electricity purchasing cost:

\[
\min J=\sum_{t=1}^{144} p_t G_t.
\]

\subsubsection{Energy Balance Constraint}

For each scheduling interval, the total electricity supply must equal the total electricity consumption. Therefore,

\[
G_t+R_t+D_t
=
L_t+C_t+W_t,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144.
\]

The left-hand side $G_t$, $R_t$, and $D_t$ represent electricity purchased from the external grid, available photovoltaic energy, and battery discharge, respectively. The right-hand side $L_t$, $C_t$, and $W_t$ represent residential demand, battery charging, and curtailed photovoltaic energy. Since electricity selling to the external grid is not considered, photovoltaic energy that cannot be consumed or stored is represented by $W_t$.
\subsubsection{Battery Dynamics}

Considering the charging and discharging efficiencies, the battery energy evolves according to

\[
S_{t+1}
=
S_t+\eta_c C_t-\frac{D_t}{\eta_d},
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144,
\]

where

\[
\eta_c=\eta_d=0.90.
\]

This constraint links the battery energy levels of consecutive scheduling intervals.


\subsubsection{Battery Capacity and Power Constraints}

The battery energy must remain within its allowable operating range:

\[
1200\le S_t\le10800,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,145.
\]

Since the maximum charging and discharging power is $5000$ kW, the corresponding maximum energy transfer in one 10-minute interval is $833.33$ kWh. Therefore,

\[
0\le C_t\le833.33,
\qquad
0\le D_t\le833.33,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144.
\]

In addition,

\[
G_t\ge0,
\qquad
W_t\ge0,
\qquad t=1,\ldots,144.
\]

\subsubsection{Initial and Terminal Conditions}

The initial battery energy at 0:00 is

\[
S_1=6000\ \text{kWh}.
\]

According to the requirement that the battery energy at 24:00 equals that at 0:00,

\[
S_{145}=S_1=6000\ \text{kWh}.
\]

This terminal condition prevents the model from reducing the daily purchasing cost simply by exhausting the battery at the end of the day.

\subsubsection{Complete LP Formulation}

Combining the objective function and all operational constraints, the complete linear programming model for Problem 1 is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\min \quad
& J=\sum_{t=1}^{144} p_t G_t \\[2mm]
\text{s.t.}\quad
& G_t+R_t+D_t=L_t+C_t+W_t,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& S_{t+1}=S_t+\eta_c C_t-\frac{D_t}{\eta_d},
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& \eta_c=\eta_d=0.9,\\
& 1200\le S_t\le10800,
&& t=1,\ldots,145,\\
& 0\le C_t\le833.33,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& 0\le D_t\le833.33,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& G_t\ge0,\quad W_t\ge0,
&& t=1,\ldots,144,\\
& S_1=S_{145}=6000.
\end{aligned}
\]

All decision variables are continuous, and both the objective function and constraints are linear. Therefore, the resulting optimization problem is a standard linear programming problem.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

The proposed linear programming model was implemented in Python and solved using the HiGHS solver through \texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog}. The solver successfully reached the optimal solution while satisfying all energy balance and battery operating constraints.

\subsubsection{Discussion}

The optimal strategy demonstrates the role of the battery in shifting electricity consumption across different price periods. During low-price periods, additional electricity is purchased and stored in the battery. When the electricity price rises, the stored energy is released to reduce expensive grid purchases. This behavior is particularly evident during the afternoon and evening peak-price periods, when substantial battery discharge occurs.

The stored energy remains within the prescribed range of 1200--10800 kWh throughout the day. The battery reaches relatively high energy levels after periods of charging and is gradually discharged when electricity prices increase. Near the end of the day, the battery is recharged so that its energy returns to 6000 kWh at 24:00.

Overall, the obtained schedule reflects a typical peak-shaving and valley-filling strategy: the microgrid shifts electricity purchases from high-price periods to low-price periods through battery storage while maintaining the required energy balance and operational constraints.


\section{Model 2: Solution to Question 2}
\label{sec:model2}

\subsection{Extension of the Basic Model}

\subsubsection{Decision structure}

Problem 2 replaces the deterministic input curves of Model 1 with uncertain daily load and PV generation. The microgrid must commit to purchases before observing either curve. Insufficient purchases expose it to expensive emergency supply, while excessive purchases create unused energy. Consequently, forecast accuracy alone is insufficient: the relevant outcome is expenditure after the purchase schedule has been executed.

We construct a central forecast, represent its uncertainty through historical daily residuals, and solve a scenario-based purchasing problem. Nodewise nonanticipativity constraints limit how early the planning model can adapt its storage actions to different scenarios. Only the purchase schedule is committed; actual storage operation follows a causal feedback rule.

\subsubsection{Central forecast and daily residual scenarios}

Load and PV generation have different temporal structures. We represent weekly demand patterns by the load observed one week earlier and recent PV levels by a median of the available observations. For the evaluation dates, define
\[
\mathcal A_{d,t}=\begin{cases}
\{d-7,\ldots,d-1\},&t=1,\ldots,143,\\
\{d-7,\ldots,d-2\},&t=144.
\end{cases}
\]
The last interval of row $d-1$ is omitted because it has not ended at the midnight decision. The central forecast is therefore
\begin{equation}
\widehat P^{N,0}_{d,t}
=P^L_{d-7,t}
-\operatorname{median}\{P^{PV}_{j,t}:j\in\mathcal A_{d,t}\}.
\label{eq:q2-central-forecast}
\end{equation}
This parsimonious baseline combines a weekly load pattern with a recent PV level. It does not explicitly predict the next day's weather.

Storage responds to the duration and timing of a deficit, not just its magnitude. We therefore retain complete historical residual paths instead of sampling individual intervals independently. To match the implementation, distinguish the current forecast from the retrospective reference used to center historical row $j$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 B^N_{j,t}&=P^L_{j-7,t}
 -\operatorname{median}\{P^{PV}_{j-7,t},\ldots,P^{PV}_{j-1,t}\},\\
 r_{j,t}&=P^{N,\mathrm{act}}_{j,t}-B^N_{j,t}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q2-residual}
\end{equation}
Only rows in $\mathcal H_d=\{\max(7,d-56),\ldots,d-2\}$ are eligible, with January 1 indexed by zero for this selection rule. Their entire reference curves and realized residuals are known at decision $d$. Unlike the current forecast, the historical reference uses all seven preceding rows, including their final entries. Thus $r_{j,t}$ is a retrospective deviation from that reference, not necessarily the error of a forecast actually issued at midnight on $j$.

We select same-weekday residual rows first, in reverse chronological order, then fill the remaining places with the most recent other eligible rows. The incomplete row $d-1$ is not patched and reused as a full residual path. Twelve selected rows yield
\begin{equation}
P^{N,(s)}_{d,t}=\widehat P^{N,0}_{d,t}+r_{j_s,t},
\qquad s=1,\ldots,12,\qquad \pi_s=\frac1{12}.
\label{eq:q2-scenarios}
\end{equation}
At the beginning of the evaluation period, the history window is shortened to the eligible rows available. The scenarios preserve empirical within-row dependence around the current forecast; their equal weights are a modeling approximation rather than calibrated confidence probabilities.

\subsubsection{Scenario-tree purchasing model}

A scenario is a possible purchase-row trajectory, not information known at 00:00. We divide the 144 modeled intervals into six stages of 24 intervals each. Under Section~\ref{sec:q2-time}, these four-hour stages begin at 00:10, 04:10, and so on; they are distinct from the civil-day blocks used in the storage reporting tables. All scenarios share the initial node. At each subsequent boundary, scenarios within each parent node are split by two-cluster K-means using their net-load trajectories over the preceding stage. Singleton nodes remain unchanged. These nested partitions contain at most twelve nodes.

Let $\mathcal T_k=\{24(k-1)+1,\ldots,24k\}$ and let $s\sim_k s'$ mean that two scenarios share a stage-$k$ node. We impose
\begin{equation}
C^{(s)}_{d,t}=C^{(s')}_{d,t},\qquad
D^{(s)}_{d,t}=D^{(s')}_{d,t},
\quad t\in\mathcal T_k,\quad s\sim_k s'.
\label{eq:q2-nonanticipativity}
\end{equation}
Storage actions can differ only after the corresponding histories have been separated by the tree. These constraints enforce nonanticipativity on a coarsened information structure, following the multistage stochastic-programming framework \cite{birge2011}. The purchase schedule $G_{d,t}$ remains identical across all scenarios throughout the day.

The daily planning problem is
\begin{equation}
\min\
\sum_t p_tG_{d,t}
+\lambda\sum_s\pi_s\sum_t5p_t\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t}
+\epsilon\sum_s\pi_s\sum_t(C^{(s)}_{d,t}+D^{(s)}_{d,t}),
\label{eq:q2-objective}
\end{equation}
subject to \eqref{eq:q2-nonanticipativity} and, for every scenario and interval,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G_{d,t}+D^{(s)}_{d,t}+\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t}
&=\Delta tP^{N,(s)}_{d,t}+C^{(s)}_{d,t}+W^{(s)}_{d,t},\\
S^{(s)}_{d,t}
&=S^{(s)}_{d,t-1}+\eta_cC^{(s)}_{d,t}-D^{(s)}_{d,t}/\eta_d,\\
S_{\min}\le S^{(s)}_{d,t}&\le S_{\max},\\
0\le C^{(s)}_{d,t}&\le\bar C,\qquad 0\le D^{(s)}_{d,t}\le\bar D,\\
G_{d,t},\widetilde E^{(s)}_{d,t},W^{(s)}_{d,t}&\ge0,\\
S^{(s)}_{d,0}&=S_{d,0},\qquad S^{(s)}_{d,144}\ge S_{\mathrm{res}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q2-planning-constraints}
\end{equation}
Here, $S_{d,0}$ denotes stored energy observed at civil midnight when row $d$ is committed. The surrogate uses this state to initialize its first modeled interval, even though that interval begins at 00:10. It omits the intervening 00:00--00:10 operation belonging to row $d-1$, so its initial condition approximates, rather than predicts, the physical 00:10 state. The terminal reserve $S_{\mathrm{res}}=3000$ kWh applies at the end of the surrogate row, 00:10 on the following date; it is not an actual civil-midnight constraint.

The weight $\lambda$ calibrates simulated shortages: $\lambda=1$ uses the actual emergency tariff, while larger weights encourage more conservative purchases. Storage capacity, power, efficiencies, the stated initial energy, and the fivefold emergency tariff are problem inputs. The 12 scenarios, 56-row window, four-hour stages, 3000 kWh planning reserve, and $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ CNY/kWh are fixed modeling choices. The selected $\lambda=0.9$ corresponds to an internal shortage price of $4.5p_t$ and was chosen through exploratory backtests on the evaluation period. It is an empirical calibration of the planning approximation, not a change to settlement or an independently validated parameter.

The objective and constraints form a linear program solved with HiGHS \cite{huangfu2018}. Shortage and surplus are scenario balancing variables and may differ within a node. The program is a surrogate for selecting purchases, not an executable storage schedule: besides approximating the initial 00:10 state, it does not explicitly prohibit emergency-assisted charging or simultaneous charge and discharge in its scenario trajectories. Its terminal reserve is therefore not guaranteed on an actual path. Only $G_{d,t}$ is passed to the physical controller below, which enforces the operating rules. We evaluate the resulting feedback expenditure, rather than treating the optimized surrogate objective as the realized cost; Section~\ref{sec:q2-information-audit} assesses the consequences of this distinction.

\subsubsection{Causal execution and settlement}
\label{sec:q2-execution}

For a physical interval $i$ assigned to purchase-row entry $(d,t)$, let $S_i^{-}$ be its actual initial stored energy and define
\[
N^{\mathrm{act}}_{d,t}=\Delta tP^{N,\mathrm{act}}_{d,t},
\qquad M_{d,t}=G_{d,t}-N^{\mathrm{act}}_{d,t}.
\]
For $M_{d,t}\ge0$, the battery absorbs the available surplus:
\begin{equation}
C_{d,t}=\min\left\{M_{d,t},\bar C,
\frac{S_{\max}-S_i^{-}}{\eta_c}\right\},\quad
D_{d,t}=E_{d,t}=0,\quad W_{d,t}=M_{d,t}-C_{d,t}.
\label{eq:q2-feedback-surplus}
\end{equation}
For $M_{d,t}<0$, it supplies as much of the deficit as its limits allow:
\begin{equation}
D_{d,t}=\min\left\{-M_{d,t},\bar D,
\eta_d(S_i^{-}-S_{\min})\right\},\quad
C_{d,t}=W_{d,t}=0,\quad E_{d,t}=-M_{d,t}-D_{d,t}.
\label{eq:q2-feedback-deficit}
\end{equation}
The realized storage state is advanced in physical chronological order:
\begin{equation}
S_i^{+}=S_i^{-}+\eta_cC_{d,t}-D_{d,t}/\eta_d,
\qquad S_{i+1}^{-}=S_i^{+}.
\label{eq:q2-actual-storage}
\end{equation}
This feedback rule uses current measurements and stored energy. It prevents simultaneous charging and discharging, confines emergency purchases to the remaining deficit, and respects storage limits. It is a feasible operating rule, not a claim of optimal real-time control. Equation \eqref{eq:q2-settlement} settles the realized trajectory using the actual fivefold emergency tariff.

At midnight on date $d$, the procedure observes stored energy, constructs the scenarios, solves \eqref{eq:q2-objective}--\eqref{eq:q2-planning-constraints}, and commits purchase row $d$. It then executes the already committed final interval of row $d-1$ over 00:00--00:10, followed by intervals 1--143 of row $d$ through midnight. Row $d$'s last interval remains pending until after the next midnight decision. Together with the availability restrictions in scenario construction, this ordering keeps the day-ahead commitment separate from subsequent measurements. Actual storage states are carried forward without resetting them between purchase rows.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol and Benchmarks}

To evaluate the purchasing strategy, we compare six policies under the same midnight information boundary, January warm-up, February--December evaluation rows, physical feedback controller, and fivefold emergency tariff. Each policy carries its own realized storage state forward after the common February initial state. Table~\ref{tab:q2-policy-definitions} identifies their forecasting and planning differences.

For a quantile benchmark, the current point forecast is
\begin{equation}
\widehat P^{N,\tau}_{d,t}
=b_{d,t}+Q_\tau\{P^{N,\mathrm{act}}_{j,t}-B_{j,t}:j\in\mathcal H_d\}.
\label{eq:q2-quantile-benchmark}
\end{equation}
The direct version uses $b_{d,t}=P^N_{d-7,t}$ and $B_{j,t}=P^N_{j-7,t}$. The separate version uses the availability-aware forecast in \eqref{eq:q2-central-forecast} and the retrospective reference in \eqref{eq:q2-residual}, respectively. Both draw residuals only from complete eligible rows. For one interval without storage or salvage value, underage and overage costs are $4p_t$ and $p_t$, giving a critical quantile of 0.8. This explains the cost-sensitive $Q_{0.8}$ benchmark without asserting its optimality for the storage-coupled problem.

\subsubsection{Matched Comparison and Cost Interpretation}
\label{sec:q2-matched-comparison}

The cost decomposition in Figure~\ref{fig:q2-model-comparison} is important: minimizing emergency expenditure alone does not minimize the total. Fixed-action scenarios achieve the lowest emergency expense, CNY 0.5594 million, but commit enough additional scheduled energy to raise their total above the tree's. The model must balance advance purchasing against the residual shortage risk left after storage operation.

To assess the role of the information constraints under matched planning settings, we next compare the tree with its two-stage relaxation. Both share scenario construction, terminal reserve, internal shortage multiplier 4.5, and execution rules. The relaxation costs CNY 14.0944 million, compared with CNY 13.8932 million for the tree. The tree spends CNY 450,882.36 more on scheduled energy but CNY 652,052.02 less on emergency energy, reducing total expenditure by CNY 201,169.65, or 1.43\%. In this experiment, the policy with nodewise restrictions therefore achieves lower feedback expenditure.

This does not contradict the LP relaxation principle: under identical inputs, removing constraints cannot worsen the optimized surrogate objective. The realized comparison instead executes each resulting schedule through feedback and carries its own storage state into subsequent days. Scenario-specific planning trajectories need not reproduce the same benefit under that controller. The relaxation's purchase schedule remains physically executable. The experiment therefore supports the value of limiting premature storage adaptation in this feedback-based purchasing framework, while distinguishing realized expenditure from the optimized surrogate objective.

\subsubsection{Feasibility and Validation}
\label{sec:q2-information-audit}

Across the 48,096 settled intervals, the recorded maximum energy-balance residual is $1.14\times10^{-13}$ kWh and the inter-row storage-linking error is zero. Stored energy remains within 1200--10800 kWh, with neither simultaneous charging and discharging nor emergency purchases during charging. Final stored energy is 3019.16 kWh at 00:10 on January 1, 2026. These checks support the physical feasibility of the executed purchase policy.

Feasible execution does not imply that the surrogate storage trajectories are reproduced. The saved planning trajectories include charging alongside positive shortage, and scenario replays confirm differences in terminal storage and cost. Actual stored energy is below 3000 kWh at 74 of the 334 recorded end-of-civil-day boundaries: the reserve is a planning condition, not a realized daily guarantee. Appendix~\ref{app:q2-validation} reports the diagnostic counts, magnitudes, and replay conventions. The expenditure comparison above uses actual feedback throughout and does not substitute planned shortages for realized emergency purchases.

Targeted perturbation tests support midnight information availability for all six policies and the within-day feedback ordering, under the timestamp convention in Section~\ref{sec:q2-time}; details appear in Appendix~\ref{app:q2-validation}. Source timestamp semantics and the missing initial interval remain explicit assumptions. Moreover, settings were explored on the evaluation period. The results therefore establish historical feasibility and expenditure, not independently tested generalization or global optimality. A separate test period with frozen settings is needed for generalization, and the stage-length and penalty-grid experiments need to be rerun under the final information boundary before drawing sensitivity conclusions. The archived experiments in Appendix~\ref{app:q2-archived-sensitivity} are not used to rank the final policies or establish their robustness.

\subsection{Detailed Validation of the Final Policy}
\label{app:q2-validation}

\textbf{Planning--execution diagnostics.}
The saved surrogate trajectories contain no simultaneous charge/discharge, but 15,200 scenario intervals contain both charging and a positive shortage variable. These trajectories are not physically executed. Replaying all twelve scenarios on each evaluation row with the actual feedback rule gives 478 terminal states below the planning reserve out of 4,008 scenario-row replays. The maximum absolute difference between planned and replayed terminal energy is 5392.32 kWh.

Each replay uses the same purchase row, scenario, and nominal initial state as its planning problem. The maximum absolute per-scenario purchase-plus-shortage cost difference is CNY 21,650.08, evaluated with the internal $4.5p_t$ shortage coefficient on both paths and excluding the throughput regularizer. This is neither an actual $5p_t$ settlement difference nor the gap between optimized expected objectives. These separate row-level replays do not constitute a continuously simulated alternative year. They quantify the surrogate approximation rather than provide an independent performance test.

\textbf{Information-availability tests.}
The implementation observes storage before executing the pending midnight interval and excludes that interval's unavailable measurement from forecasting. Targeted tests perturb entry 144 of row $d-1$ as well as rows $d$ onward; scenarios and the midnight purchase plans remain unchanged in the recorded tests. The comparison suite reports the corresponding purchase-plan boundary checks for all six policies. A separate within-day test changes later realized measurements and leaves earlier feedback actions unchanged. These checks support the implemented information ordering under the adopted timestamp convention, not the timestamp assumption itself or generalization to an unseen period.
